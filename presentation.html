01

Understanding
intelligence

Before we use it well, we should be precise about what it is — and what it is not. Most bad decisions about AI start with a category error here.

Understanding intelligence01 · Definition

Human intelligence

The full spectrum of cognitive, emotional, social and physical capacities that let a person understand the world, learn from very few examples, and make value-based decisions in ambiguous situations.

Capacity

Reasoning

Logic, abstraction, and cause-and-effect thinking that generalises to situations never seen before.

Capacity

Emotion

Recognising and managing feelings, in yourself and in the person across the table.

Capacity

Creativity

Original thought and genuine invention, not only recombination of what already exists.

Capacity

Judgement

Deciding what should be done, not merely what can be done. This is the one that carries accountability.

A child touches a hot stove once and avoids fire for life. One example, permanent generalisation, no dataset.
You see a colleague's face and know something is wrong before a word is said — an emotional read and a judgement call in the same instant.
4
Understanding intelligence01 · Definition

Artificial intelligence

Computer systems that perform tasks once thought to require human thinking — by learning statistical patterns from very large datasets and using them to predict the next best output.

Property

Pattern-based

Learns correlations from billions of examples. It is extraordinarily good at what resembles what it has seen.

Property

Speed and scale

Runs continuously, consistently, across many tasks at once, without fatigue or mood.

Property

Narrow

Excellent at the shape of task it was trained for, and unreliable a short distance outside it.

Property

Dependent

Needs human framing, evaluation and correction. It has no independent way to know whether it succeeded.

A spam filter saw millions of junk messages, learned the patterns, and now sorts new mail in milliseconds — far beyond any person's capacity.
The same class of system drafts an email instantly and fluently, and may confidently invent a name, a figure or a citation that does not exist.
5
Understanding intelligence01 · Side by side

Two different kinds of mind

Not better and worse. Different in kind — which is precisely why the division of labour between them is a design decision worth making deliberately.

DimensionHuman intelligenceArtificial intelligence
Learns fromLived experience and a handful of examplesEnormous datasets and millions of examples
StrengthDepth and meaning — knowing what mattersSpeed and scale — knowing what, and how fast
CreativityOriginal intent and directionRecombining existing patterns fluently
EthicsMoral agency and accountabilityNo moral agency — it optimises against data
NatureGeneral, contextual, and slow to scaleNarrow, literal, and scales instantly
Failure modeTired, biased, inconsistent — and usually knows itConfident, fluent, and wrong — and cannot tell
The division that follows from this table: AI handles the what and the how fast. People handle the why and what matters. Every failure in section 07 is a case of that line being crossed.
6
Understanding intelligence01 · The key insight

"Jagged" intelligence

Capability is not a smooth line from simple to hard. Even the strongest 2026 frontier models solve doctoral-level problems one moment and fail at something a child handles the next — and nothing in the output signals which you just got.

Why it happens

Fluency feels like understanding, but the model has no model of truth — only of language. It predicts what a correct-sounding answer looks like. When the pattern is right, the answer is right. When the pattern is right and the fact is wrong, the answer still sounds right.

What it means for us

You cannot delegate on the basis of difficulty. "This task is easy, so it will be fine" is not sound reasoning about a jagged system. You delegate on the basis of whether the output can be checked — which is the whole argument of section 05.

It writes complex, working code across several files — and miscounts the letters in a short word.
Asked for a source, it produces a real-looking book and author that have never existed.

The sentence to carry through the rest of this deck

AI is a remarkably capable tool — not a colleague with judgement. It amplifies your thinking; it does not take over your responsibility. Keep a person in the loop wherever the answer has consequences.

Where we are02 · Tool generations

Three generations of tooling — all still in the building

Most teams are running a mix. Knowing which generation a tool belongs to tells you how much supervision it needs.

2021 – 2023

Autocomplete

Inline next-line suggestions. You stay in the driver's seat the entire time. Low risk, low ceiling.

Copilot inline, Tabnine, Codestral FIM

2024 – 2025

Chat & IDE assistants

Ask, paste, apply a diff. The model sees files you point it at. You still assemble the context by hand.

Cursor, Cline, Copilot Chat, Continue

2026 — now

Coding agents

Plan, edit across files, run the test suite, read the failure, fix it, repeat. Sessions run for hours. Fleets run in parallel.

Claude Code, Codex, Antigravity CLI, Muse Code, Devin

The consequence for us

When the agent can run the code, the bottleneck moves off "can it write the function" and onto what the agent is allowed to see, run and merge. That is an engineering problem, not a prompting problem — and it is the subject of section 03.

04

Prompt
engineering

The craft of telling a model exactly what you need — clearly, with the right context, in a way that can be repeated and tested.

Prompt engineering04 · The mindset shift

It is a workflow now, not a bag of tricks

In 2026 the job is not finding magic words. It is assembling the right context — role, task, constraints, examples and output format — so the model can reliably do the thing you need, again tomorrow, for someone else.

Principle

Cleverness in wording is over

Cleverness in context assembly is the craft that replaced it. What the model can see matters far more than how you phrase the ask.

Principle

Treat prompts like code

Version them, test them, review the changes. A prompt that matters to the business belongs in the repository, not in someone's chat history.

Principle

Context beats phrasing

Relevant context yields far larger gains than any rewording ever will. If output is poor, ask what the model could not see — not how to say it more nicely.

"Write me a post" → "Write an Instagram post for a new café: friendly tone, three lines, one emoji, and a clear call to visit."
"Fix this code" → "This Python raises a TypeError on line 10; it should sum a list. Fix it and explain the cause briefly."

Prompt engineering04 · Anatomy

The six building blocks

Any prompt that has to work more than once should have all six. Missing blocks are where inconsistent output comes from.

01

Role

Who the model should act as.

02

Task

The job, in one clear line.

03

Context

The data, documents or facts it needs.

04

Constraints

Boundaries, rules, length limits.

05

Examples

One to five samples of the pattern you want.

06

Format

The exact shape of the output.

quarterly-summary
Role:      Senior financial analyst.
Task:      Summarise the Q3 report for the board.
Context:   <report> ...the actual figures... </report>
Limits:    Only figures from the report. Max 120 words.
Format:    3 bullets + a one-line risk callout.
ticket-triage
Role:      Support lead for an ERP rollout.
Task:      Classify this client ticket and route it.
Context:   Teams: finance, inventory, integrations.
Example:   "Invoice total wrong" → finance · high.
Format:    team · priority · one-line reason.

Prompt engineering04 · What goes wrong

Six mistakes that account for most bad output

Worth reading aloud to the team once. Every one of them is common, and every one is cheap to stop doing.

01

Everything in one mega-prompt

Build complexity through iteration. It is a conversation partner, not a search box.

02

Being vague

"Make it better" gives it nothing to aim at. Name the goal and the audience.

03

Stacking ten "do nots"

Prefer positive instructions. A pile of negatives degrades quality; use them sparingly.

04

Dumping everything in

More context is not better context. Curate high-signal material instead of drowning the task.

05

Magic filler phrases

"Take a deep breath" and similar folklore do not help. Clear structure does.

06

Iterating on vibes

Without a test set you cannot tell whether a change helped or just moved the error somewhere you did not look.

Vagueness: "write me a plan" returns a generic plan, because you never said the topic, the timeline or the goal.
Dumping: fifty pages pasted in and one question asked — the answer drowns, when the one relevant paragraph would have been enough.

Prompt engineering04 · From draft to reliable

How to refine a prompt

Start simple, then diagnose what is actually missing rather than guessing at rewordings.

01

Start plain

A clear, direct prompt with no examples yet. See what you get.

→
02

Diagnose the gap

Missing instruction? Missing context? Missing example? Name which one.

→
03

Add the matching fix

Examples for pattern. Steps for reasoning. A format spec for reuse.

→
04

Test and version

Run it against a small fixed set of cases. Keep what wins; record what you changed.

Translation: too formal → add "casual tone" → now too long → add "one short sentence". Three cycles, then it is reliable.
Email reply: too cold → paste one past reply you liked as an example → it now matches your voice without further instruction.

The habit that separates a team that improves from one that guesses

Even a twenty-example evaluation set is worth more than a week of impression-based tweaking. Loop until the prompt is reliable, not until it is impressive once.

05

How to actually
use it

The teams getting real output are not using better models. They are engineering the context the model works inside.

Practice05 · Three levels

Automation → agentic workflow → agent

Three different things, routinely confused, with very different costs and failure modes. Naming the level correctly is half of scoping a project.

Level 1 · rigid

Automation

Predefined, rule-based steps. The same input produces the same output every time. Ideal for high-volume, predictable work where correctness is non-negotiable.

When a form is submitted, send this email. Post the ledger entry on the first of the month.

Level 2 · hybrid

Agentic workflow

Deterministic rails orchestrate the flow, with model-powered steps dropped in at the genuinely fuzzy decision points. Predictable and flexible — the sweet spot for most business systems today.

Extract vendor data with a model, then route approval through fixed rules. Classify complaints, route each by existing policy.

Level 3 · autonomous

AI agent

The model decides actions at runtime. It reasons, picks tools, adapts, and persists until the goal is met rather than until a script ends. Most capable, least predictable, hardest to audit.

"Keep this client from churning" — it monitors, follows up, escalates.

Notice that almost everything valuable we deliver to clients sits at level 2. Level 1 is what we already build. Level 3 is where the demos live — and where the audit trail disappears.
22
Practice05 · The decision rule

Which one should you build?

One question decides it: can the logic be written down?

Build a workflow

When inputs are structured and the logic can be codified

And whenever you need an audit trail, regulatory defensibility, or a result that must be identical every single time. Payroll is the canonical case: the rules are fixed and the answer must be right on every run, not most runs.

Build an agent

When inputs are ambiguous and the logic resists codification

When the task genuinely needs interpretation and adaptation at runtime. Answering varied, unpredictable support tickets is the canonical case: every one is different and needs to be understood before it can be routed.

The rule of thumb

Start with the simplest option that works. Add agent reasoning only where branching logic would become unmaintainable. Most real systems end up combining both — the agent interprets the ambiguity, the workflow enforces the consistency — and that combination is usually the right answer rather than a compromise.

Practice05 · Context engineering

Prompt engineering → context engineering

The unit of work stopped being the prompt. It is now the whole environment the agent sees: rules, tools, memory, scope, and the checks that run without being asked.

BASE RULES

AGENTS.md

What the project is, how it is built, what is forbidden

GUARDRAILS

Hooks

Lint, tests and blocks that run deterministically

REFERENCE

Skills

Reusable procedures loaded only when relevant

DELEGATION

Subagents

Isolated scopes, parallel work, clean contexts

TASK SCOPE

Spec

The acceptance criteria the work is checked against

Why this became necessary in 2026

Sessions now run for hours, so context has to survive compaction, overflow and restarts. And running five to fifty agents in parallel multiplies whatever your context setup is — good or bad.

The one-line version

Good context engineering is what makes agents consistently useful rather than occasionally impressive. Every hour spent here is repaid across every session afterwards.

Practice05 · AGENTS.md

AGENTS.md: the de facto standard, usually done badly

A study of 466 open-source projects accepted to MSR 2026 scanned 10,000 active repositories: only about 5% had any AI context file, and half of those were never updated after the first commit.

  • Descriptive — where things live, what the project is.
  • Prescriptive — the workflow to follow: build commands, commit format, review steps.
  • Prohibitive — hard boundaries: never edit generated files, never push to main.
  • Explanatory — why a rule exists, so the agent generalises correctly.
  • Conditional — what to do in specific situations.
Every line should map to a failure you have actually seen. A blank or copy-pasted file is worse than none, because it sets the agent's expectations wrongly.

Keep it at the repository root, commit it, and treat it as code: when a rule stops being true, the file is wrong and the agent will act on the wrong rule. The most common sections across the study were conventions, contribution rules and architecture.

# AGENTS.md

## Project
Odoo 18 addons for pharmacy retail. Python 3.11,
Postgres 16. Modules live in addons/.

## Commands
Test:   pytest -q addons/<module>/tests
Lint:   ruff check . && pylint-odoo addons/
Run:    ./odoo-bin -c dev.conf -u <module>

## Rules
- Never edit files under */migrations/.
- Never push to main. Branch: feat/<ticket>.
- XML views: keep xpath, never replace whole arch.
- Every model field change needs a migration
  script — we broke production twice on this.

## Definition of done
Tests pass, ruff clean, migration written,
PR body cites the spec file.

Practice05 · Spec-driven development

Vibe-code the prototype. Spec-drive everything that ships.

Spec-driven development became practical only once regenerating an implementation from a description got cheap. The spec, not the code, becomes the durable artefact.

01

Specify

Requirements and acceptance criteria, in a committed file

→
02

Clarify

Let the agent surface ambiguity before it guesses

→
03

Plan

Architecture, data model, API contracts, migration path

→
04

Tasks

Atomic units with one objective and an acceptance check

→
05

Verify

Does the output satisfy the criteria? Review against the spec

The constitution file

One place for project rules every change must respect — dependency policy, architectural boundaries, security requirements — consulted by each workflow step instead of restated in every spec. GitHub Spec Kit is the MIT-licensed default if we want structure without buying a product; it installs into an existing agent rather than replacing it.

Habits that make it stick

Cite the spec in commits and PR bodies. Treat specs as durable docs that outlive the generated code. Use pre-flight checklists for security, accessibility and observability. A good task list reads like something a junior engineer could execute — which is roughly what an agent is.

Practice05 · Plumbing

MCP, skills, subagents, hooks

Four mechanisms. Together they are the difference between an agent that guesses about your system and one that can look.

Connect

MCP — Model Context Protocol

The standard way to expose tools and data to an agent. Datadog's server went GA in March 2026 for live logs, metrics and traces inside Claude Code, Cursor, Codex and Copilot; Chrome DevTools MCP gives agents real page inspection and debugging.

Reuse

Skills

A SKILL.md is a procedure the agent loads when relevant — house style, review checklist, deployment runbook. The format was adopted by Codex CLI as an open standard, and marketplaces now ship packs spanning multiple harnesses.

Scale

Subagents & parallelism

Isolated contexts for scoped jobs — scan a migration, generate tests, review a diff. Combined with git worktrees this is how teams run several agents without them colliding.

Enforce

Hooks

Deterministic checks the agent cannot talk its way past: run the linter before "done", block writes to protected paths, require the test suite to be green. Guardrails belong in code, not in prose.

One measured example of what connection buys: a cross-file refactor that took 96 tool calls and 84 minutes on a baseline setup took 5 tool calls and 4.4 minutes once the agent had MCP access to code intelligence.

Practice05 · Applied to our stack

What this looks like on our codebases

Concrete mapping for the work we actually do — Odoo/Python, Spring Boot services, and the client integration projects.

Do this

  • AGENTS.md per repository, with the real odoo-bin / Maven commands, the module layout, and the migration rule we have broken before.
  • Hooks that run ruff, pylint-odoo and the test suite before an agent is allowed to report success.
  • Read-only MCP access to a staging Postgres replica, to Sentry/logs, and to code search. Read-only is the whole point.
  • Subagents for the boring, high-volume work: version-migration scans, XML view diffs, test backfill, translation files.
  • A shared skill pack for our conventions — QWeb patterns, ORM cache rules, invoice/RTL PDF pipeline, API contract style.

Never this

  • Production database credentials in an agent's context, under any framing, including "just to read".
  • Client data pasted into a consumer chat tier where the terms allow training on inputs.
  • Agent-authored migrations merged unread. Migrations are the one place a subtle error is unrecoverable.
  • Auto-approved shell or git operations on anything that can reach a remote.
  • Dependencies added by an agent without checking the package actually exists and is the one it claims to be.

Automation
platforms

Zapier, Make and n8n. The tools that actually build the level-one and level-two systems from the previous section — and the ones our clients will name.

Automation platforms06 · The category

What these platforms are, and why they matter to us

All three do the same fundamental job: connect software that was never designed to talk to each other, without writing and maintaining integration code. All three have now added AI and agent features on top.

The core job

Trigger → steps → action

Something happens in one system, a sequence of steps runs, something changes in another. A form submission creates an invoice. A payment updates the CRM. A file lands and gets filed.

The new layer

Model-powered steps

Each platform now lets you drop a reasoning step into the middle of that flow — classify this message, extract these fields, decide which team handles it — which is exactly the level-two pattern from section 05.

Why it is our problem

They compete with bespoke builds

A meaningful share of what we currently quote as custom integration work is four modules on one of these platforms. Knowing when that is true protects both our margin and our credibility.

The honest framing for a client conversation

These are not toys, and they are not a replacement for engineering either. They are the right tool when the work is plumbing between systems that already have APIs. They become the wrong tool when the logic gets deep enough that you are effectively programming in a visual editor with no tests and no version control.

Automation platforms06 · Zapier

Zapier — the broadest, the simplest, the priciest

The oldest of the three and still the default name. Its whole design philosophy is that a non-technical person finishes a working automation in minutes.

  • Around 7,000 integrations — more than any competitor, by a wide margin. If a client uses a niche regional CRM or an obscure industry tool, Zapier is the one most likely to already support it.
  • A linear builder, not a canvas. Pick a trigger app and event, pick an action, map the fields, activate. There is genuinely nothing to learn.
  • The AI layer: create workflows from a plain-language description, drop AI steps into a flow, and use Zapier Agents — assistants that work from instructions, browse, consult attached knowledge and act across connected apps, rather than following a fixed recipe.
  • Also ships a built-in database (Tables), simple front-ends (Interfaces), chatbots, and MCP access for connecting AI tools.

Automation platforms06 · Make

Make — the visual middle ground

Formerly Integromat. A drag-and-drop canvas rather than a list of steps, which is what makes genuinely branching logic practical without writing code.

  • Several thousand integrations — fewer than Zapier, far more than n8n — plus routers, filters, iterators and error handling as first-class parts of the canvas.
  • Real logic without leaving the tool. Unlimited branching, and JavaScript or Python through a code module when the visual approach runs out.
  • AI agents since 2025, an AI toolkit, and connections to hundreds of AI services. You can use Make's own model provider, or bring your own key on the higher tiers — which matters if a client insists on a specific model or their own contract.
  • The sweet spot is operations and RevOps work: orchestrating dozens of SaaS tools with intelligent branching, without hiring a developer to maintain it.

Automation platforms06 · The cost math

Why the same workflow costs wildly different amounts

The billing unit — not the headline price — is what determines the bill. Take one ordinary workflow: ten steps, running a thousand times a month.

10,000
Zapier tasks. Every step of every run is metered, so complexity multiplies directly into cost.
10,000
Make credits. Same arithmetic — but the credits themselves cost roughly three to five times less.
1,000
n8n executions on cloud, because the run is the unit, not the step. Self-hosted, the number is irrelevant.

The pattern to internalise

Cost scales with steps × runs on Zapier and Make, and with runs alone on n8n cloud. So the more complex the workflow, the more sharply the three diverge. A simple two-step automation running occasionally is cheap everywhere. A fifteen-step scenario firing every few minutes is where the decision actually gets made.

And the AI multiplier on top

Model-powered steps are metered differently from ordinary ones on every platform, and on n8n your agent nodes bill straight through to your own model provider. Whatever the platform costs, the model calls are a separate line item — forecast them separately or the first invoice is a surprise.

Usage pricing does not get decided, it creeps. A client starts delighted at twenty dollars a month and is annoyed at four hundred eighteen months later, having approved no single increase. Model the volume at the proposal stage, not after.

The productivity numbers are real. So are the stability and security numbers. Both are in the same datasets.

Risk07 · The verification tax

AI is an amplifier, not an accelerator

DORA's finding, from qualitative analysis of enterprise software engineers alongside their survey data, is that the impact on the software lifecycle is a series of trade-offs rather than a straight-line improvement.

What speeds up

Creation

AI reliably accelerates initial code generation and reduces the friction of starting a new task. Over 80% of professionals in the DORA data believe it increased their productivity.

What absorbs it

Verification

The time saved in creation is frequently re-allocated to auditing and verifying the output. The work does not disappear; it moves downstream to review.

The result

Both directions at once

Higher AI adoption is associated with an increase in software delivery throughput and an increase in delivery instability. More code, moving faster, into pipelines and review gates built for less of it.

Why this matters for a small team

An amplifier multiplies whatever is already there. Strong foundations — tests, CI, review discipline, observability — get multiplied. So do weak ones. DORA's ROI work makes the same point: the return depends on organisational systems, not on which tool you bought.

Risk07 · Security

Security is the sharp edge

This is the area where the research is most consistent and least flattering, and where the numbers have not improved despite vendor claims.

  • Veracode tested over 100 models on security-sensitive tasks: 45% of generated samples introduced OWASP Top 10 vulnerabilities. The pass rate held flat near 55% across testing cycles from 2025 into early 2026.
  • 86% of samples failed to defend against cross-site scripting; 88% were vulnerable to log injection. Java fared worst, at a 72% failure rate.
  • Georgia Tech's Vibe Security Radar tracked CVEs directly attributable to AI coding tools: 6 in January 2026, 15 in February, 35 in March.
  • Across Fortune 50 enterprises, AI-assisted developers committed at three to four times the rate of their peers and introduced security findings at roughly ten times the rate.

New attack surface: prompt injection

CVE-2025-53773 showed hidden prompt injection in a pull request description leading to remote code execution through a coding assistant, rated 9.6. EchoLeak demonstrated data exfiltration through a Copilot integration. Any text an agent reads is untrusted input — issues, PR descriptions, dependency READMEs, fetched web pages.

The dependency problem

Agents add libraries casually, and they sometimes name packages that do not exist. Attackers register those names. Pin versions, verify provenance, and put software composition analysis in CI.

Risk07 · Guardrails

Non-negotiables

Seven rules. They cost very little to adopt now and are extremely expensive to retrofit after an incident.

01

Human gate on every merge

No PR merges unreviewed by a person, regardless of who or what wrote it. Cap PR size so the review is real rather than ceremonial.

02

Treat AI output as untrusted input

SAST, DAST and SCA in CI on every AI-touched branch. Security-relevant paths — auth, payments, file handling, query construction — get a specialist read.

03

Dependency discipline

Pin versions. Verify the package exists and is the one intended. Block new runtime dependencies without an explicit decision record.

04

Least-privilege agents

Read-only, scoped credentials. Staging replicas, never production. Approval gates on shell, git push and any network call.

05

Secrets never enter context

No .env files, no client credentials, no customer data in prompts. Scan for leaked secrets on every commit, not every release.

06

Label AI-assisted work

A commit trailer or PR tag. Without it you cannot measure whether any of this is working, and you cannot audit after an incident.

07

Keep the skill

Developers still have to be able to read, reason about and debug the code they ship. If someone cannot explain why a diff works, it does not merge — this is a competence requirement, not a policy preference, and it is the one that protects us in five years.

Claude Code08 · MCP in depth

What MCP costs, and how it is governed

Two questions come up whenever this is proposed: does connecting everything slow it down, and who decides what gets connected.

Cost

  • Idle servers are cheap. Tool search is on by default: only tool names load at session start, and the full definitions are fetched when a tool is actually needed.
  • /context all shows the bill per tool, so a server that is quietly expensive can be found and disconnected.
  • Timeouts are configurable per server, which matters for a slow internal system that would otherwise stall a session.

Governance

  • The organisation can restrict which servers exist. Allowlists and denylists control what a developer may add or connect to, and chosen servers can be pushed to everyone.
  • Servers defined in a repository require approval before they connect — cloning a project does not silently grant it integrations.
  • A session can be locked to only the servers explicitly passed to it, which is the right setting for automated runs.

Pair a connection with a skill

MCP gives the agent the ability to reach a system. A skill gives it the knowledge to use that system well — our schema, our query patterns, our message formats. The two together are far more effective than either alone.

The rule that is not negotiable

Everything an MCP tool returns is untrusted input. A ticket description, a log line or a web page can carry instructions aimed at the agent. Connect read-only, against staging replicas, with scoped credentials — never production write access.

Claude Code08 · Scale

One at a time, or many at once

The step change in output is not a faster single session. It is several running at the same time, and some running with nobody present.

Parallel

Several at once

Multiple sessions working in isolated copies of the repository so their changes cannot collide, managed from one screen. One person can supervise work that would previously have occupied several.

Unattended

In the pipeline

It runs inside CI as a step like any other: review this diff, triage this issue, translate these strings, raise a pull request. No person in the loop until there is something to approve.

Scheduled

On a timetable

Recurring jobs that run in the cloud whether or not a laptop is open, or that trigger on a repository event. Morning review passes, overnight failure analysis, weekly audits.

Five recognisable patterns. Every one of them is a habit, which means every one of them is fixable by training rather than by spending.

Open agents09 · The category

A different category from a coding agent

Claude Code works inside a repository on a task you gave it. These work across your whole life or business, continuously, reached through the chat apps you already use.

Where they live

In your messaging apps

WhatsApp, Telegram, Slack, Discord, Signal, iMessage, Teams and more, all routed through one gateway process running on hardware you control.

What they touch

Your actual systems

Shell commands, the browser, email, calendar, files. Not a sandbox with a preview — the real thing, by design.

When they run

Without being asked

A scheduler wakes them on an interval, and webhooks push events in. They act between your messages, not only in response to them.

Why a business briefing covers them at all

Three reasons. They are the most-starred software on GitHub, so our developers are already reading about them. Clients in our market will ask for "an AI agent that runs my business over WhatsApp" and we need a credible answer. And the architecture they popularised — a persistent agent with memory, skills and scheduled autonomy — is the direction the whole field is moving, whoever ends up winning.

OpenClaw

An open-source, self-hosted AI assistant created by Peter Steinberger. Launched in late January 2026, renamed twice along the way — it began as Clawdbot, then Moltbot. MIT licensed.

What it actually is

A gateway. It sits between the chat apps you already use and an AI agent with access to your machine. You message it like a person; it runs shell commands, drives a browser, reads and writes files, handles email and calendar, and reports back in the same thread. Native iOS and Android apps pair to your own gateway, with remote approval for actions.

What made it famous

A developer had it negotiate roughly $4,200 off a car purchase by managing the dealer email thread over several days. That story — an agent doing multi-day commercial work unsupervised — is what moved this category from curiosity to something businesses started asking about.

Open agents09 · OpenClaw

How OpenClaw is put together

Worth understanding in outline, because it is the reference implementation of nearly every pattern in production agent systems today — including the ones Claude Code uses.

01

The gateway

One process owns the connections to every chat channel, routes messages to the agent, and manages sessions. Channels are plugins, so adding WhatsApp or Teams is configuration rather than code.

02

Memory as files

Context and memory are markdown on local disk rather than a hosted database. That is the whole ownership argument: nothing leaves the machine except the model calls you configure.

03

Tools and skills

A portable skill format the community extends, plus a plugin marketplace with curation and trust guidance. The same idea as Claude Code skills, in a different ecosystem.

04

The heartbeat

A scheduler wakes the agent at a configurable interval so it can act unprompted, alongside cron jobs and inbound webhooks. This is what makes it feel like staff rather than software.

05

Model-agnostic

Configurable providers with failover, including local model services. It can drive Claude, or an open-weight model on your own hardware, or several with routing between them.

06

Full system access — by design

The project states it plainly: read and write files, run shell commands, execute scripts, with full access or sandboxed as your choice. Most people leave the default. Hold that thought for slide 68.

The pitch

"The agent that grows with you." It runs on your server, persists what it learns across sessions, and is meant to be measurably more useful after a month of work than on day one. It doubles as research infrastructure. Beyond automating tasks it can generate tool-use trajectories in parallel with checkpointing, and export them for fine-tuning and reinforcement-learning experiments. That is a real differentiator if a team ever wants to train on its own workflows.

Open agents09 · Hermes

The self-improvement loop, and why it is the interesting claim

Most agent frameworks treat every task as a blank slate. Hermes is built around not doing that.

01

Do the work

Execute the task through whatever tools and channels are connected.

→
02

Record outcomes

Persist what worked and what failed, rather than discarding it at the end of the session.

→
03

Find the pattern

Curate that memory and model the user, so repeated situations are recognised as repeats.

→
04

Write a skill

Generate a reusable skill from the pattern, so next time the procedure already exists.

Why this matters strategically

It is the same insight behind everything in section 05: the compounding value is not in the model, it is in the accumulated, written-down knowledge of how this organisation works. Hermes tries to accumulate it automatically. Claude Code has you write it deliberately, which is slower but auditable — you can read, review and version what it believes.

The honest caveat

Independent reviewers report real constraints: tight memory limits, a context floor, and overhead from scheduled prompting. Automatic self-improvement is a genuinely hard problem and the claim is ahead of the evidence. Treat it as a promising direction, not a delivered capability.

Open agents09 · The security reality

Read this slide before anyone installs anything

This is not criticism of either project. It is the shape of any agent that combines three things: access to private data, ingestion of untrusted content, and the ability to act outward. That combination turns prompt injection from a prompt-quality issue into an operational security problem.

What has already happened

  • January 2026: researchers at Giskard exploited a live deployment, showing paths to data exfiltration and account takeover from ordinary misconfiguration.
  • A disclosed vulnerability rated 8.8 worked like this: a crafted email asked the agent to render a link preview, and the preview request leaked the session to an attacker's domain. Further issues followed in February involving the shell capability.
  • Internet-exposed instances were probed within minutes of coming online, according to Bitsight's research — and the more capable attackers ignored the AI layer entirely and went for the exposed admin surface.
  • Attacks seen in the wild: CrowdStrike documented an injection attempt to drain crypto wallets, planted in a public post on a social network built for agents to read.

Why patching does not solve it

Indirect injection means the attacker never talks to your agent. They plant instructions in something it will read later — an email signature, a calendar invite, an issue, a web page. When the agent reads it, those instructions land in the same context as your real request, and the model has no reliable way to tell them apart. The project's own security policy lists prompt injection as out of scope for vulnerability reports, which is the industry norm — and means the mitigation is entirely yours.

Microsoft's published guidance is blunt: do not run it with your primary work or personal accounts, and do not run it on a device holding sensitive data. That is the correct starting position for us too.

Open agents09 · Our position

Where we land

Interesting, worth understanding, and not going anywhere near a client. Both statements are true at once.

Acceptable

  • A dedicated, isolated host — not anyone's working laptop, not a server holding client work.
  • Dedicated accounts created for it, with no access to company email, drives or repositories.
  • Internal-only automation: our own reminders, our own reports, our own internal tooling.
  • Never reachable from the internet. No exposed control interface, no port forwarding, authentication on by default.
  • Approval gates on actions, and every inbound message treated as untrusted.

Not acceptable

  • Any client data, ever — including a client's email thread, ticket system or documents.
  • Production credentials of ours or anyone else's, in any form, under any framing.
  • Selling it as a deliverable to a client who wants "an AI agent on WhatsApp". We can build that on governed infrastructure; we cannot hand over a self-hosted agent with system access and walk away.
  • Running it on the machine where delivery work happens.
The commercial angle is real and worth naming: clients in our market will ask for this. Being the firm that can explain exactly why the naive version is dangerous — and then deliver a governed version instead — is a stronger position than either refusing outright or saying yes and finding out.
